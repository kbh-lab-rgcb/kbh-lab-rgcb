title: Breaking the stromal barrier to pancreatic cancer treatment
tag: Pancreatic cancer · Tumour microenvironment
lead: By disrupting the S1PR1/STAT3 signalling loop, the multiple sclerosis drug
  FTY720 made pancreatic tumours more responsive to gemcitabine in preclinical
  models and weakened the fibrotic microenvironment that protects them.
why: This strategy treats the tumour and its protective niche as one therapeutic
  problem, using S1PR1/STAT3 blockade to weaken desmoplasia and improve
  chemotherapy response at the same time.
excerpt: FTY720 disrupted an S1PR1/STAT3 signalling loop that supports pancreatic
  tumour growth, desmoplasia and drug resistance. Combined with gemcitabine it
  shrank tumours and weakened the fibrotic microenvironment in preclinical
  models.
papers: 10.7150/thno.25308

Pancreatic cancer is difficult to treat not only because of the cancer cells
themselves, but because tumours are surrounded by a dense, fibrotic stroma known
as desmoplasia. This tissue barrier supports persistent oncogenic signalling,
restricts drug response and contributes to resistance to gemcitabine. Our study
focused on a reinforcing loop between sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1)
and the transcription factor STAT3 — two signals that connect tumour growth with
the surrounding microenvironment.

We tested FTY720, or fingolimod, an approved treatment for multiple sclerosis
that can act as a functional antagonist of S1PR1. In human and mouse pancreatic
cancer cells, FTY720 inhibited growth, increased apoptosis, induced cell-cycle
arrest and enhanced the effects of gemcitabine. The combination disrupted
mitochondrial membrane potential, weakened the S1PR1/STAT3 loop, and inhibited
the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition associated with aggressive disease.

In mouse models the combination reduced tumour size and increased tumour-cell
death while suppressing NF-κB, S1PR1/STAT3 and Sonic hedgehog signalling. It
also reduced extracellular-matrix deposition and activation of pancreatic
stellate cells, both key features of desmoplasia; altered proteins involved in
gemcitabine transport and metabolism; and restored activity of the tumour
suppressor PP2A. The result is a proof of principle for drug repurposing:
targeting a lipid-signalling circuit may both attack pancreatic cancer cells and
loosen the stromal defences that make chemotherapy less effective. Clinical
translation would still require dedicated evaluation of dosing, safety and
immune effects in patients with cancer.
